What fits between two infinite walls?

Lesson 22 used the Schrödinger equation to evolve a state. Here the physical walls give us exact standing-wave solutions. The integer n counts how many half-wavelengths fit across the well. Its internal nodes are the positions where the corresponding eigenfunction vanishes.

un(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)

En = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²),   n = 1, 2, 3, …

L · Width

The walls are at x = 0 and x = L. At fixed n and m, doubling L quarters En.

n · Level and nodes

The wave fits n half-wavelengths. An eigenstate has n − 1 internal nodes at x = jL/n, for j = 1 through n − 1.

√(2/L) · Normalization

The sine squared integrates to L/2. The amplitude multiplier √(2/L) gives ∫|un|² dx = 1.

m · Mass

The kinetic operator is −(ℏ²/2m)d²/dx². At fixed n and L, doubling m halves the energy.

ℏ · Reduced Planck constant

ℏ = h/(2π). It sets the energy scale and converts energy to angular frequency through E/ℏ.

Show Me Why · derive the states and detector probability

1. Solve inside the well. With V = 0, the stationary equation is −(ℏ²/2m)ψ″ = Eψ. For E > 0 set k² = 2mE/ℏ². The general solution is ψ(x) = A sin(kx) + B cos(kx), where A and B are constants.

2. Apply both walls. At x = 0, ψ = B = 0. At x = L a nonzero A requires sin(kL) = 0. Thus kL = nπ, and the wavelength is 2L/n.

3. Test zero energy. At E = 0 the equation gives ψ″ = 0 and ψ = Ax + B. The first wall gives B = 0 and the second gives AL = 0, so A = 0 as well. The zero function has squared norm zero. For this kinetic Hamiltonian, integration by parts with ψ = 0 at both walls gives E = (ℏ²/2m)∫|ψ′|² dx for a normalized eigenstate. This is positive for every physical state here, so the ladder starts at n = 1.

4. Normalize the wave. Using sin²z = (1 − cos 2z)/2 gives ∫0L sin²(nπx/L) dx = L/2. Take the real phase convention A = √(2/L). Amplitude has units length−1/2, and density has units length−1.

5. Read off energy and nodes. Substitute k = nπ/L into E = ℏ²k²/(2m). The walls and the internal positions jL/n have zero amplitude. A zero of the real trace alone at one time does not imply a node, because the imaginary part can be nonzero.

6. Integrate the detector area. Put y = x/L. Then |un(x)|² dx = 2 sin²(nπy) dy. Integrating from 0 to y gives the cumulative probability Fn(y).

Fn(y) = y − sin(2nπy)/(2nπ)

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = Fn(b/L) − Fn(a/L)

Worked example · the middle third

Subtract Fn(1/3) from Fn(2/3). For n = 1, the result is 1/3 + √3/(2π) ≈ 60.90%. For n = 2, it is 1/3 − √3/(4π) ≈ 19.55%.

The first detector covers the tallest part of the ground-state density. The second straddles the node at L/2. The node has zero density, while a finite-width bin around it can include nearby positions with positive density.

Stationary density and moving interference

One eigenstate evolves as Ψn(x,t) = un(x) exp(−iEnt/ℏ). Its phase factor has magnitude one, so its density stays |un|² while Re Ψ and Im Ψ change. Positive and negative lobes differ by π and share a single global rotation.

Ψ(x,t) = cos α ur(x) exp(−iErt/ℏ)

    + sin α us(x) exp(iφ−iEst/ℏ)

δ(t) = φ − (Es−Er)t/ℏ

Here 0 ≤ α ≤ π/2 sets the two amplitude magnitudes, φ is the initial phase of the second component relative to the first, and r and s are distinct. Orthonormality eliminates the integrated cross term over the full well. The norm and the sum of the two energy probabilities both equal cos²α + sin²α = 1.

|Ψ|² = cos²α ur² + sin²α us² + 2 cos α sin α urus cos δ(t)

T = 2πℏ / |Es−Er|

The interference term varies across the well and in time. With both components occupied, the relative phase advances through a full cycle in the beat period T. For equal positive components with r = 1 and s = 2, the left-half probability goes from 1/2 + 4/(3π) at t = 0 to 1/2 at T/4 and 1/2 − 4/(3π) at T/2.

How the lab integrates and samples a superposition

The cumulative cross term is the integral of 2 sin(rπy) sin(sπy) from 0 to y. The product-to-sum identity gives

Jrs(y) = sin[(r−s)πy]/[(r−s)π] − sin[(r+s)πy]/[(r+s)π]

Combine cos²α Fr(y), sin²α Fs(y), and 2 cos α sin α cos δ Jrs(y). Differences give detector and histogram-bin probabilities. To sample a position, draw a uniform cumulative probability and invert the cumulative function by bisection. The answer is independent of plot resolution.

The reference units stay fixed. Lref and mref set Eref = π²ℏ²/(2mref Lref²) and tref = ℏ/Eref. The numerical energy is Eₙ/Eref = n²/[(m/mref)(L/Lref)²]. The ladder’s ratio to its current first level stays n² even as the absolute energies change.
