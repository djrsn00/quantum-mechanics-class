Lesson 13 · Quantum measurement

Measurement and the Born Rule

You prepare the same state 100 times. One measurement setting predicts outcome 1 with probability 64%. Another predicts outcome + with probability 98%. How can both predictions be right, and what will a single measurement actually show?

The preparation specifies the state. The apparatus setting specifies which basis you measure in. Each run gives one outcome from that setting. Here we use an ideal projective measurement with two outcomes, each associated with one basis ket.

An amplitude is the coefficient along a measurement ket

Write the prepared ket as |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The amplitudes α and β are the coordinates in the ordered orthonormal basis (|0⟩, |1⟩). The opening preparation is normalized because (3/5)² + (4/5)² = 1.

If outcome a is associated with the basis ket |a⟩, its amplitude is ca = ⟨a|ψ⟩. This inner product picks out the coefficient of |ψ⟩ along |a⟩.

Calculate the complex inner product and square the magnitude

Let a0 and a1 be the coordinates of |a⟩ in the same 0/1 basis. The bra ⟨a| is the conjugate transpose of |a⟩. This turns the column into a row and replaces each entry with its complex conjugate. The star indicates conjugation. For example, (u + iv)* = u − iv, where u and v are real and i² = −1.

ca = ⟨a|ψ⟩ = a0*α + a1*β

Apply the Born rule after computing the amplitude. Even when the amplitude is negative or complex, the probability is a nonnegative real number.

P(a) = |ca|² = ca*ca = (u − iv)(u + iv) = u² + v²

A complete orthonormal measurement basis expands the entire state. When you take the inner product of that expansion with itself, each cross term vanishes and the remaining terms add up to the squared norm. For this normalized state, Σa |ca|² = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1.

The same state gives different predictions in a different basis

In Z, the two outcome kets are |0⟩ and |1⟩. In X, they are |+⟩ and |−⟩. These are alternative apparatus settings. Compare them using systems prepared in the same state.

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
|−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

Work through the opening state in Z and X

In Z, take one coefficient at a time. The opening amplitudes are 3/5 and 4/5, so the probabilities are 9/25 and 16/25.

In X, each outcome receives contributions from both coefficients. The signs in the basis kets determine whether those contributions add or subtract.

c+ = (α + β)/√2 = (3/5 + 4/5)/√2 = 7/(5√2)
c− = (α − β)/√2 = (3/5 − 4/5)/√2 = −1/(5√2)

Square the magnitudes to get P(+) = 49/50 = 98% and P(−) = 1/50 = 2%. The minus amplitude is negative and its probability is positive.

Predict what changes when 4/5 becomes 4i/5

Only the relative phase changes. Before looking at the calculation, decide whether the Z probabilities and the X probabilities should change. Try the Imaginary coefficient preset in both settings.

|ψi⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4i/5)|1⟩

Z stays at 36% and 64%. In X, the amplitudes are (3 + 4i)/(5√2) and (3 − 4i)/(5√2). Each has squared magnitude (3² + 4²)/50 = 1/2. As in Lesson 12, combine the amplitudes before squaring their magnitudes.

One outcome, many trials

A single measurement reports a single label. With the opening preparation in X, plus is likely and minus can still occur. One click cannot recover an unknown state because many preparations can produce the same outcome.

The Born rule predicts the distribution. A finite simulated run gives counts, and the observed frequency is the count divided by the number of trials. For example, if exactly 98 of 100 trials give plus, one more minus gives a plus frequency of 98/101, about 97.03%. That extra trial moves the observed frequency farther from 98%.

Remeasure the same system or prepare another?

In this ideal nondegenerate projective measurement, each outcome corresponds to a single basis ket. Once the outcome is a, the measured system is in |a⟩ up to a global phase. Repeat the same measurement immediately on that system, with no intervening evolution or disturbance, and it reports a with certainty.
