Lesson 13 · Quantum measurement

Measurement and the Born Rule

You prepare the same state 100 times. One measurement setting predicts outcome 1 with probability 64%. Another predicts outcome + with probability 98%. How can both predictions be right, and what will a single measurement actually show?

The preparation specifies the state. The apparatus setting specifies which basis you measure in. Each run gives one outcome from that setting. Here we use an ideal projective measurement with two outcomes, each associated with one basis ket.

An amplitude is the coefficient along a measurement ket

Write the prepared ket as |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The amplitudes α and β are the coordinates in the ordered orthonormal basis (|0⟩, |1⟩). The opening preparation is normalized because (3/5)² + (4/5)² = 1.

If outcome a is associated with the basis ket |a⟩, its amplitude is ca = ⟨a|ψ⟩. This inner product picks out the coefficient of |ψ⟩ along |a⟩.

Calculate the complex inner product and square the magnitude

Let a0 and a1 be the coordinates of |a⟩ in the same 0/1 basis. The bra ⟨a| is the conjugate transpose of |a⟩. This turns the column into a row and replaces each entry with its complex conjugate. The star indicates conjugation. For example, (u + iv)* = u − iv, where u and v are real and i² = −1.

ca = ⟨a|ψ⟩ = a0*α + a1*β

Apply the Born rule after computing the amplitude. Even when the amplitude is negative or complex, the probability is a nonnegative real number.

P(a) = |ca|² = ca*ca = (u − iv)(u + iv) = u² + v²

A complete orthonormal measurement basis expands the entire state. When you take the inner product of that expansion with itself, each cross term vanishes and the remaining terms add up to the squared norm. For this normalized state, Σa |ca|² = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1.

The same state gives different predictions in a different basis

In Z, the two outcome kets are |0⟩ and |1⟩. In X, they are |+⟩ and |−⟩. These are alternative apparatus settings. Compare them using systems prepared in the same state.

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
|−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

Work through the opening state in Z and X

In Z, take one coefficient at a time. The opening amplitudes are 3/5 and 4/5, so the probabilities are 9/25 and 16/25.

In X, each outcome receives contributions from both coefficients. The signs in the basis kets determine whether those contributions add or subtract.

c+ = (α + β)/√2 = (3/5 + 4/5)/√2 = 7/(5√2)
c− = (α − β)/√2 = (3/5 − 4/5)/√2 = −1/(5√2)

Square the magnitudes to get P(+) = 49/50 = 98% and P(−) = 1/50 = 2%. The minus amplitude is negative and its probability is positive.

Predict what changes when 4/5 becomes 4i/5

Only the relative phase changes. Before looking at the calculation, decide whether the Z probabilities and the X probabilities should change. Try the Imaginary coefficient preset in both settings.

|ψi⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4i/5)|1⟩

Z stays at 36% and 64%. In X, the amplitudes are (3 + 4i)/(5√2) and (3 − 4i)/(5√2). Each has squared magnitude (3² + 4²)/50 = 1/2. As in Lesson 12, combine the amplitudes before squaring their magnitudes.

One outcome, many trials

A single measurement reports a single label. With the opening preparation in X, plus is likely and minus can still occur. One click cannot recover an unknown state because many preparations can produce the same outcome.

The Born rule predicts the distribution. A finite simulated run gives counts, and the observed frequency is the count divided by the number of trials. For example, if exactly 98 of 100 trials give plus, one more minus gives a plus frequency of 98/101, about 97.03%. That extra trial moves the observed frequency farther from 98%.

Remeasure the same system or prepare another?

In this ideal nondegenerate projective measurement, each outcome corresponds to a single basis ket. Once the outcome is a, the measured system is in |a⟩ up to a global phase. Repeat the same measurement immediately on that system, with no intervening evolution or disturbance, and it reports a with certainty.

Tilted Detector

Z and X aren’t the only settings. This detector measures in the basis |θ⟩ = cos θ|0⟩ + sin θ|1⟩ and |θ⊥⟩ = −sin θ|0⟩ + cos θ|1⟩. θ = 0° is Z and θ = 45° is X. Turn θ, watch P(θ) = |⟨θ|ψ⟩|², and record the settings you find.

    Show the math

    For a real state |ψ⟩ = cos φ|0⟩ + sin φ|1⟩, the amplitude is c_θ = ⟨θ|ψ⟩ = cos θ cos φ + sin θ sin φ = cos(θ − φ), so P(θ) = cos²(θ − φ) and P(θ⊥) = sin²(θ − φ). Outcome θ is certain when the detector lines up with the state (θ = φ) and impossible a quarter-turn away. Turning the detector by 180° only flips the sign of |θ⟩, a global phase, so θ and θ + 180° are the same measurement. A complex state such as |+i⟩ gives |e^(iθ)|²/2 = 1/2 for every real θ.

    Choose the pair |0⟩, |+⟩ and find a state where their squared overlaps happen to add to exactly 1. The checker reads your setting.

    Show the math

    Expand the state in an orthonormal basis: |ψ⟩ = c_a|a⟩ + c_b|b⟩ with c_a = ⟨a|ψ⟩ and c_b = ⟨b|ψ⟩. Then ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = |c_a|² + |c_b|² + (cross terms that contain ⟨a|b⟩ = 0), so the probabilities add to ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1. If ⟨a|b⟩ ≠ 0, the cross terms don’t vanish and the sum depends on the state. If a ket is longer than 1, it inflates every overlap. Either way the numbers are not probabilities. A single state that happens to give 1 proves nothing. Completeness is a statement about every state.

    Show the math

      Show the math

      With N fresh copies and P(0) = p, the expected count is Np, and the observed frequency is count ÷ N. Runs show a pattern: when N grows 100-fold, the typical distance between the count and Np grows only about 10-fold, roughly like √N. Dividing by N, the frequency’s typical distance from p shrinks roughly like 1/√N. That is why frequencies settle toward the probability even though counts wander.       What counts can’t tell✓ Complete

      Which property of a state can no number of Z, X and Y counts reveal?

      Show the math

      Write |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with α real and ≥ 0, and β = |β|e^(iφ). Then P(0) = α², P(+) = 1/2 + α|β| cos φ and P(+i) = 1/2 + α|β| sin φ. Z gives α.         Error Detective

        Each case file holds a worked solution with exactly one planted mistake. Find the first line that goes wrong, and name the mistake. The diagram shows what the solution claims next to what is actually true.

        Error Detective controls

        Case 1 · Square first✓ Complete

        Find P(+) for |ψ⟩ = (1/2)|0⟩ − (√3/2)|1⟩.

        1. In X, c₊ = ⟨+|ψ⟩ = (α + β)/√2, with α = 1/2 and β = −√3/2.
        2. |α|² = 1/4 and |β|² = 3/4.
        3. So P(+) = (|α|² + |β|²)/2 = (1/4 + 3/4)/2 = 1/2.
        4. Then P(−) = 1 − P(+) = 1/2.
        Case 2 · More than certain✓ Complete

        Find P(+) for |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩.

        1. c₊ = (α + β)/√2 = (√3/2 + 1/2)/√2 = (√3 + 1)/(2√2).
        2. P(+) = |c₊|² = (√3 + 1)²/4 = (4 + 2√3)/4 ≈ 1.866.
        3. So P(−) = 1 − 1.866 = −0.866.
        Case 3 · Missing its own state✓ Complete

        Find P(+i) for |ψ⟩ = |+i⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2.

        1. |+i⟩ has coordinates (1, i)/√2 in the 0/1 basis.
        2. So the bra is ⟨+i| = (⟨0| + i⟨1|)/√2.
        3. c₊ᵢ = ⟨+i|+i⟩ = (1·1 + i·i)/2 = (1 − 1)/2 = 0.
        4. So P(+i) = 0.
        Case 4 · Seven out of ten✓ Complete

        An unknown state gave 0 in 7 of 10 fresh Z trials. Find P(0).

        1. The observed frequency of 0 is 7/10 = 0.7.
        2. So P(0) = 0.7 exactly, and |α| = √0.7.
        3. So 100 more trials will give exactly 70 zeros.
        Case 5 · One plus✓ Complete

        A single X measurement of an unknown state gave +. Predict a Z measurement on a fresh copy.

        1. Outcome + happened, so c₊ = ⟨+|ψ⟩ ≠ 0.
        2. So the prepared state was |+⟩.
        3. So a fresh copy gives 0 in Z with probability |⟨0|+⟩|² = 1/2.
        Case 6 · The next one✓ Complete

        A source prepares |30°⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. One system gives 1 in Z. Predict Z for the next system from the source.

        1. P(1) = (1/2)² = 1/4 for each system from the source.
        2. After outcome 1, that measured system is in |1⟩.
        3. So the next system from the source gives 1 with certainty.
        4. Therefore P(0) = 0 for the next system.
        Show the math

        Four checks catch most measurement errors. Combine amplitudes before you square: P(a) = |⟨a|ψ⟩|². Conjugate the bra. Every probability lies between 0 and 1, and in one basis they add to 1. A finite run gives frequencies that estimate the probabilities, and one outcome only rules out states with zero amplitude for it. Finally, ask which system is measured: a measured system sits in its outcome’s ket, while a fresh copy is in the prepared state.

        Show the math

        A state cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ)sin(θ/2)|1⟩ sits θ from the top at longitude φ. Points on the sphere carry half-angles, so states 180° apart on the sphere are orthogonal. For a measurement whose outcome ket |a⟩ sits at the a end of the axis, |⟨a|ψ⟩|² = cos²(γ/2), where γ is the angle on the sphere between |ψ⟩ and |a⟩. Z is the vertical axis, X runs through |+⟩ and |−⟩, and Y through |+i⟩ and |−i⟩. After outcome a the state is |a⟩, the end of the axis.

        Quantum Random Number Generator

        Measure fresh copies of |+⟩ in Z and each outcome is a fair random bit. Tilt the state and the bits become biased. Von Neumann’s pairing trick repairs the bias: read the raw bits in pairs, output 0 for 01 and 1 for 10, and discard 00 and 11.

        Repair the bias✓ Complete

        With P(1) = 0.9 and pairing on, generate at least 1000 raw bits. What do you see in the paired output?

        Show the math

        Each raw bit is an independent Z measurement of a fresh copy, with P(1) = p = sin²t. A pair reads 01 with probability (1 − p)p and 10 with probability p(1 − p). These are equal, so the kept output is fair for every p, as long as the raw bits are independent. A pair is kept with probability 2p(1 − p), so the output rate is p(1 − p) bits per raw bit: 1/4 at best, when p = 1/2.

        Show the math

        With empty arms, the path amplitudes (upper, lower) = (e^(iθ)/√2, 1/√2) recombine at splitter 2, so P(D0) = cos²(θ/2) and P(D1) = sin²(θ/2). At θ = 0, D1 is dark. A live bomb measures the path. With probability 1/2 the photon is found in the lower arm and the bomb explodes. Otherwise the photon is left in the upper path alone, and splitter 2 sends it to D0 or D1 with probability 1/4 each. Without the lower path nothing cancels at D1, so a D1 click flags a live bomb. Repeating on D0 gives P(D1 eventually) = (1/4)/(1/4 + 1/2) = 1/3.
