The One-Dimensional Schrödinger Equation

A wave packet is heading toward a barrier taller than its central kinetic energy. What do you expect a detector on the far side to record?

What determines the next instant?

iℏ ∂Ψ(x,t)/∂t = −(ℏ²/2m) ∂²Ψ(x,t)/∂x² + V(x,t)Ψ(x,t)

Position x and time t label the complex amplitude Ψ. The imaginary unit i obeys i² = −1, and ℏ is the reduced Planck constant. The mass m sets the strength of the kinetic term. The potential energy V is fixed for each run in the lab.

Time derivative ∂Ψ/∂t

This is the instantaneous rate of change of the amplitude at a position. Divide the right side by iℏ to read the rate directly.

Kinetic term −(ℏ²/2m)∂²Ψ/∂x²

Lesson 21’s momentum operator is p̂ = −iℏ∂/∂x. Applying it twice and dividing by 2m gives the kinetic term. The curvature here is the second derivative of the complex amplitude. Two packets can have the same density and opposite momenta because their spatial phases differ.

Mass m

For fixed momentum, a larger mass reduces the free packet’s velocity p₀/m. It also decreases the coefficient ℏ²/(2m) multiplying the curvature.

Potential term VΨ

The obstacle controls set the potential energy at each grid point. Adding this term to the kinetic operator gives the Hamiltonian H, so the equation reads iℏ∂Ψ/∂t = HΨ.

Initial packet Ψ(x,0)

The center x₀ sets the launch position, σ sets its initial density width, and p₀ sets the spatial phase gradient p₀/ℏ. These controls prepare the state at t = 0.

Position density |Ψ|²

Square the real and imaginary parts and add them. The probability of a position in a detector interval is the integral of this density across the interval.

What evolves, and what is measured?

The lab starts with a localized Gaussian. Its envelope fixes the initial density, and its phase sets the central momentum p₀.

Ψ(x,0) = (2πσ²)−1/4 exp[−(x−x₀)²/(4σ²)] exp[ip₀(x−x₀)/ℏ]

The Schrödinger equation evolves these amplitudes deterministically. A position measurement at a specified time is random. The chance for an interval [a,b] is ∫ab|Ψ(x,t)|² dx before conditioning on numerical survival. The evolving density describes the distribution across repeated preparations, while a single measurement gives one position.

Worked example · free translation and spreading

With V = 0, the center moves as x₀ + p₀t/m. Each momentum component has its own velocity, so the density width grows as σ(t) = σ√[1 + (ℏt/(2mσ²))²].

Select Free with the opening packet. At t = 5 T₀, the ideal free center moves from −15 to −5 L₀ because p₀/m = 2 L₀/T₀. The width grows from 3 to 3√[1 + (5/18)²] L₀ ≈ 3.11 L₀. Watch the peak move and the profile widen.

Incident, reflected, and transmitted components

The approaching packet is incident. After encountering the barrier, an outgoing component on the entrance side is reflected and one moving away on the far side is transmitted. During overlap, the two region totals also include whatever part of the incident packet is still approaching and interference can change the local density.

For the opening preset, m = 1 m₀, p₀ = 2 ℏ/L₀, and σ = 3 L₀. The central kinetic energy is K₀ = p₀²/(2m) = 4/2 E₀ = 2 E₀. The initial momentum width is Δp = ℏ/(2σ) = (1/6)ℏ/L₀. Since ⟨p²⟩ = p₀² + Δp², the mean initial kinetic energy is [4 + (1/6)²]/2 E₀ ≈ 2.0139 E₀.

A finite packet contains a range of energies. A sub-barrier component decays inside the forbidden region, yet it can have a nonzero transmitted amplitude after a finite-width barrier. That is tunneling. The packet can also have an above-barrier tail. For this narrow opening momentum distribution, the continuum threshold is |p| = √6 ℏ/L₀, about 2.7 momentum standard deviations above p₀ on the positive side. The above-barrier weight is small. Comparing the separated far-side mass with that weight and with the launch tail gives more information than comparing V with K₀ alone.

Why are some states stationary?

For time-independent V, try Ψ(x,t) = ψ(x)T(t), where T(t) is the time factor. Substitute and divide by ψT where the product is nonzero.

iℏ (dT/dt)/T = [−(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² + Vψ]/ψ = E

−(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² + Vψ = Eψ

T(t) = exp(−iEt/ℏ), up to a constant

The left side depends only on time and the right side only on position, so both equal the same separation constant E. With the physical boundary conditions, E is an energy eigenvalue. One eigenstate keeps the same density because its time factor is a phase of magnitude one. A superposition of different energies can develop a changing density as the relative phases evolve.

At an ideal infinite wall the wavefunction must vanish. In Lesson 23 you’ll use physical walls to determine the allowed energies of a well. Here the finite computational domain uses absorbing edge layers and zero endpoint values so outgoing packets can leave the useful part of the grid.

Numerical units, grid, and probability accounting

Reference length L₀ and mass m₀ define units of momentum ℏ/L₀, energy E₀ = ℏ²/(m₀L₀²), and time T₀ = m₀L₀²/ℏ. In these units the numerical equation uses ℏ = 1 and the mass control sets m/m₀.

The grid runs from −60 to +60 L₀ with Δx = 0.10 L₀ and zero endpoint amplitudes. For κ = ℏ²/(2mΔx²), the centered second derivative gives a Hamiltonian with diagonal 2κ + Vⱼ and neighboring entries −κ.

(I + iΔtH/2ℏ) Ψⁿ⁺¹ = (I − iΔtH/2ℏ) Ψⁿ

Each Crank–Nicolson step solves the complex tridiagonal system at fixed Δt = 0.01 T₀. The initial state is normalized by Σ|Ψⱼ|²Δx. After each solve, a smooth numerical mask multiplies the amplitude by exp[−3Δt((|x|−45)/15)⁴] where |x| > 45 in scaled units. The readout adds the measured loss from each mask application. The state keeps its surviving norm as it evolves.

The grid resolves the packet and the permitted obstacles at finite precision. Region values sum grid-point masses, so sharp edges are represented to the grid spacing. Inspect scattering before the outgoing packets enter the absorbing bands for the clearest comparison.
