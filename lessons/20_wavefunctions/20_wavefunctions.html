From amplitude to detector probability

In a discrete basis, the state gives an amplitude for each listed outcome. In the position representation, ψ(x) = ⟨x|ψ⟩ is a complex amplitude at each position x. Its real part u and imaginary part v describe the state at the chosen time. The plotted curves show amplitudes at positions, with detections recorded separately as individual positions.

ψ(x) = u(x) + iv(x),   ψ*(x) = u(x) − iv(x)

ρ(x) = ψ*(x)ψ(x) = (u − iv)(u + iv) = u² + v²

Here i is the imaginary unit and * denotes complex conjugation. The position probability density ρ has units nm⁻¹, so ψ has units nm⁻¹ᐟ². In a narrow bin of width Δx measured in nm, P(bin) ≈ ρ(x)Δx when the density varies little within the bin. Probability is dimensionless.

Sum over small bins and take the limit as the bin widths approach zero. Write X for the measured random position and x for the coordinate of integration. The resulting integral gives the chance that a detector with edges a and b captures the particle.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab ρ(x) dx

∫−∞∞ |ψ(x)|² dx = 1

For these continuous states, the integral over an exact point is zero even at the peak of the density. Integrating over all possible positions gives the normalization condition on the second line.

A normalized Gaussian

Use a real packet amplitude with center μ and density standard deviation σ > 0, both measured in nm. Choose the amplitude scale A so the integral of the density is 1.

gμ(x) = A exp[−(x − μ)²/(4σ²)]

A = (2πσ²)−1/4

ρμ(x) = exp[−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)]/(σ√(2π))

Derive the Gaussian normalization

Squaring the amplitude gives |A|² exp[−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)]. Substitute z = (x − μ)/σ, so dx = σ dz. The full-line limits stay −∞ and +∞.

1 = |A|²σ ∫−∞∞ exp(−z²/2) dz

= |A|²σ√(2π)

Solving for the positive real A gives (2πσ²)−1/4.

Worked example · halve the detector width

For μ = 0 nm and σ = 1 nm, the center density is 1/√(2π) ≈ 0.39894 nm⁻¹. A detector covering [−1, 1] nm has probability 68.27%. Halving the window to [−0.5, 0.5] nm gives 38.29%.

The two windows have the same center density. The narrower window retains more than half the probability because it keeps the densest part of the packet. If both windows are narrow enough that ρ is nearly constant across them, halving the width gives approximately half the probability.

Two coherent packets

Put the same-width Gaussian amplitudes gL and gR at −1 and +1 nm. The separation is d = 2 nm. Their real overlap is s = ∫gLgR dx = exp[−d²/(8σ²)] over the full line. Assign the right term phase φ relative to the left and normalize the sum.

ψφ(x) = [gL(x) + exp(iφ)gR(x)]/√D

D = 1 + 1 + 2s cos φ = 2 + 2s cos φ

Re ψ = (gL + gR cos φ)/√D

Im ψ = gR sin φ/√D

ρ = (gL² + gR² + 2gLgR cos φ)/D

The two squared terms integrate to 1 each. The cross term integrates to 2s cos φ, which sets the phase-dependent normalization. A fixed prefactor of 1/√2 would give a squared norm of 1 + s cos φ. The packet terms add as coherent amplitudes, and a detection records one position.

In the lab, select two coherent packets and place the probe at 0 nm. Try 0°, 90°, and 180°. At 90° the imaginary part is nonzero. At 180° the equal midpoint amplitudes cancel and the density has a node. A window centered there can still collect probability from nearby positions.

How the lab integrates an interval

Let Φ be the standard normal cumulative distribution function. For one packet, the interval probability Fμ is obtained by subtracting the cumulative probabilities at the two edges.

Fμ(a,b) = Φ((b − μ)/σ) − Φ((a − μ)/σ)

Multiplying the two offset Gaussian amplitudes combines the exponents into −x²/(2σ²) − d²/(8σ²). Thus gLgR = sg0². The coherent-state interval is the corresponding sum of three Gaussian intervals.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = [F−1(a,b) + F+1(a,b) + 2s cos φ F0(a,b)]/D

The lab approximates Φ numerically for the full-line Gaussian. The result depends on the detector edges and the preparation, independent of how many points are drawn in the plots.
