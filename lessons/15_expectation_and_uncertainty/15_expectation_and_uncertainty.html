Find the balance point

Imagine placing weights of 0.64 at −1 and 0.36 at +1. The balance point lies to the left of zero. The Born rule supplies the weights for the opening preparation |ψ⟩ = (3|0⟩ + 4i|1⟩)/5. In Z, |0⟩ corresponds to +1 and |1⟩ corresponds to −1, so their probabilities are 9/25 and 16/25.

⟨Z⟩ = (+1)(9/25) + (−1)(16/25) = −7/25 = −0.28

The expectation value is the mean of the distribution. The number −0.28 answers what the readings average to over many freshly prepared systems. A single reading still lands on one of the two circles.

Connect the weighted average to the operator

Let A be the observable, a one of its eigenvalues, and P(a) the Born probability of that reading. The sum adds one term for each possible eigenvalue. The ket |ψ⟩ represents the normalized preparation, and ⟨ψ| is its conjugate transpose.

⟨A⟩ = Σa aP(a) = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩

In this complete nondegenerate orthonormal eigenbasis, write A = Σa a|a⟩⟨a|. Each |a⟩ is the normalized eigenket associated with reading a. Insert that expression between the preparation bra and ket. Each term becomes a⟨ψ|a⟩⟨a|ψ⟩ = a|⟨a|ψ⟩|². The squared overlap is the Born probability from Lesson 13.

Measure the spread around the mean

In the opening distribution, +1 is 1.28 above the mean and −1 is 0.72 below it. The probability-weighted signed deviations cancel. Squaring the deviations lets both distances contribute positively to the spread.

(9/25)(1.28) + (16/25)(−0.72) = 0

The variance is the probability-weighted average of the squared deviations. Its square root is the standard deviation, written ΔA. It describes the spread of repeated readings in the chosen preparation.

(ΔZ)² = (9/25)(1.28)² + (16/25)(−0.72)² = 576/625
ΔZ = √(576/625) = 24/25 = 0.96

The dashed span marks mean ± one standard deviation. In this example it runs from −1.24 to 0.68, so the possible reading +1 lies outside it. The span illustrates a distance scale for the distribution.

Derive the second-moment formula

Write μ = ⟨A⟩ for the mean. The identity operator I leaves each ket unchanged. Subtracting μI from A shifts each eigenvalue to its deviation a − μ.

(ΔA)² = Σa P(a)(a − μ)² = ⟨(A − μI)²⟩ = ⟨A²⟩ − μ²

Expanding the square gives ⟨A²⟩ − 2μ⟨A⟩ + μ²⟨I⟩. Normalization makes ⟨I⟩ = 1, leaving ⟨A²⟩ − μ². Here ⟨A²⟩ averages the squares of the readings. μ² squares the average reading.

Every Pauli observable has A² = I, so ⟨A²⟩ = 1. For the opening Z setup, the shortcut gives 1 − (−7/25)² = 1 − 49/625 = 576/625.

Keep the preparation and change the observable

Choose |0⟩ and measure Z. Every simulated reading is +1. The mean is +1 and the spread is zero. Keep |0⟩ selected and switch to X. Both readings now have probability 1/2, the mean is zero, and the spread is one.

Quantum uncertainty here describes the distribution for a particular observable and state. Even with an ideal instrument and a known pure preparation, the spread can be positive.

The sample mean uses the observed counts. If n+ and n− count the two outcomes and N is their total, then ā = (n+ − n−)/N when N is positive. A fair X sample with five of each sign has mean zero. If the next reading is +1, the mean moves to 1/11, farther from the theoretical zero. Larger samples generally improve estimates across repeated experiments. The preparation’s intrinsic ΔA stays fixed.

Compare the spreads of two observables

The Robertson relation gives a lower bound on the product of two spreads. Prepare two groups of systems in the same state. Measure A on one group and B on the other. Each spread describes its own distribution.

ΔA · ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A, B]⟩|
[A, B] = AB − BA

The bracket [A, B] is the commutator. It subtracts the two products formed in opposite orders. For X and Y, [X, Y] = 2iZ, where i² = −1. The factor ½ and the magnitude of 2i give the lower bound |⟨Z⟩|.

Show the math

The beam balances when the weighted signed distances cancel: Σ P(a)(a − f) = 0. Because Σ P(a) = 1, that gives f = Σ a·P(a) = ⟨A⟩. The balance point need not be a reading. With readings −1 and 3 at weights 3/4 and 1/4 it is 0, a value no trial shows.

    If A|ψ⟩ = a|ψ⟩, every trial reads a, so ⟨A⟩ = a and (ΔA)² = ⟨(A − aI)²⟩ = 0. Conversely, (ΔA)² = ‖(A − ⟨A⟩I)|ψ⟩‖², which vanishes only when A|ψ⟩ = ⟨A⟩|ψ⟩.     How Many Trials?

    A finite run of N trials gives a sample mean ā = (n₊ − n₋)/N, which lands near ⟨A⟩ but rarely on it. Over many repeated runs, ā spreads out by about ΔA/√N. Lesson 13 saw counts spread like √N. Here you use the same fact to plan an experiment. “Repeat 100 experiments” simulates 100 separate labs and doesn’t use your budget. “Run one experiment” is your own lab, with a budget of 2000 trials.

    Each trial gives a reading with mean ⟨A⟩ and variance (ΔA)². The sample mean of N independent trials has variance (ΔA)²/N, so its spread is ΔA/√N. The preparation’s own ΔA never shrinks with N.

      For √p|0⟩ + e^(iφ)√(1 − p)|1⟩: ⟨Z⟩ = 2p − 1, ⟨X⟩ = 2√(p(1 − p)) cos φ and ⟨Y⟩ = 2√(p(1 − p)) sin φ. Each Pauli spread is √(1 − mean²), so a target spread is a target on the size of a mean. Two targets fix two of the three means, up to sign. The third mean follows from ⟨X⟩² + ⟨Y⟩² + ⟨Z⟩² = 1, and must not need more than the 1 that is available.

      Error Detective

      Each case file holds a worked solution with exactly one planted mistake. Find the first line that goes wrong, and name the mistake. The diagram shows what the solution claims next to what is actually true.

      Error Detective controls

      Case 1 · The likeliest reading✓ Complete

      Prepare (2|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√5 and measure Z. What will the next reading most likely be?

      1. P(+1) = 4/5 and P(−1) = 1/5.
      2. ⟨Z⟩ = 4/5 − 1/5 = 3/5.
      3. So the next reading will most likely be 0.6.
      4. Over 100 trials, about 60 readings will show 0.6.
      Case 2 · No spread at all✓ Complete

      Readings 0 and 2 each have probability 1/2. Find ΔA.

      1. ⟨A⟩ = 0·(1/2) + 2·(1/2) = 1.
      2. ⟨A²⟩ = ⟨A⟩² = 1.
      3. (ΔA)² = ⟨A²⟩ − ⟨A⟩² = 1 − 1 = 0.
      4. So every reading equals 1.
      Case 3 · A better meter✓ Complete

      Prepare (2|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√5 and measure X with an ideal instrument.

      1. ⟨X⟩ = 2 Re(α*β) = 4/5.
      2. (ΔX)² = 1 − (4/5)² = 9/25, so ΔX = 3/5.
      3. ΔX = 3/5 is the instrument’s error bar, so a better instrument would shrink it.
      4. With a perfect instrument every reading would be 4/5.
      Case 4 · Exactly the bound✓ Complete

      A state has ⟨Z⟩ = 1/2 and ΔX = √3/2. What can you say about ΔY?

      1. For X and Y, ΔX·ΔY ≥ ½|⟨[X, Y]⟩| = |⟨Z⟩|.
      2. Here |⟨Z⟩| = 1/2.
      3. So ΔX·ΔY = 1/2, which gives ΔY = (1/2)/(√3/2) = 1/√3.
      4. So every state with these values has ΔY ≈ 0.577.
      Case 5 · One batch for both✓ Complete

      Estimate ΔX and ΔY for a preparation, using 2000 systems.

      1. Prepare 2000 systems in the same state.
      2. Measure X on each system and record the readings.
      3. Then measure Y on those same 2000 systems and record those readings.
      4. Use the two lists to compute ΔX and ΔY for the preparation.
      Case 6 · Twice the bound✓ Complete

      Find the X·Y lower bound for a state with ⟨Z⟩ = −0.4.

      1. [X, Y] = 2iZ.
      2. So ⟨[X, Y]⟩ = 2i⟨Z⟩ = −0.8i.
      3. So ΔX·ΔY ≥ |⟨[X, Y]⟩| = 0.8.
      4. A state with ΔX·ΔY = 0.6 would therefore break the relation.
      Show the math

      A mean is an average over many trials, not a reading. ⟨A²⟩ averages squared readings, and it is ⟨A⟩² only when ΔA = 0. ΔA is a property of the state and the observable, and it is still there with a perfect instrument. The Robertson relation is an inequality with ½ and a magnitude. Each spread is estimated from its own fresh systems, because measuring X first changes the state that Y would see.

The distance from that point to the X axis is √(⟨Y⟩² + ⟨Z⟩²) = √(1 − ⟨X⟩²) = ΔX, and the same goes for Y and Z. Three means fix the point, and the point fixes the state up to a global phase. P(0) = (1 + ⟨Z⟩)/2 and φ = atan2(⟨Y⟩, ⟨X⟩). The opposite point negates every mean and keeps every spread.

      Instrument Noise vs Quantum Spread

      A real meter adds its own random error. Each displayed value here is the quantum reading +1 or −1 plus independent noise with spread σ. Independent spreads add in quadrature, so the observed variance is (ΔA)² + σ². Turn the noise down to zero and watch what is left.

      Show the math

      Displayed value = reading + noise, with the noise independent of the reading and averaging to zero. Variances of independent parts add: (observed spread)² = (ΔA)² + σ². A better instrument lowers σ and can never remove ΔA. For an eigenstate ΔA = 0, so all of the observed spread is instrument noise. To reduce the quantum spread you change the state, not the meter.
