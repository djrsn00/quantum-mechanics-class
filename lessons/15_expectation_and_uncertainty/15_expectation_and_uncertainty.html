Find the balance point

Imagine placing weights of 0.64 at −1 and 0.36 at +1. The balance point lies to the left of zero. The Born rule supplies the weights for the opening preparation |ψ⟩ = (3|0⟩ + 4i|1⟩)/5. In Z, |0⟩ corresponds to +1 and |1⟩ corresponds to −1, so their probabilities are 9/25 and 16/25.

⟨Z⟩ = (+1)(9/25) + (−1)(16/25) = −7/25 = −0.28

The expectation value is the mean of the distribution. The number −0.28 answers what the readings average to over many freshly prepared systems. A single reading still lands on one of the two circles.

Connect the weighted average to the operator

Let A be the observable, a one of its eigenvalues, and P(a) the Born probability of that reading. The sum adds one term for each possible eigenvalue. The ket |ψ⟩ represents the normalized preparation, and ⟨ψ| is its conjugate transpose.

⟨A⟩ = Σa aP(a) = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩

In this complete nondegenerate orthonormal eigenbasis, write A = Σa a|a⟩⟨a|. Each |a⟩ is the normalized eigenket associated with reading a. Insert that expression between the preparation bra and ket. Each term becomes a⟨ψ|a⟩⟨a|ψ⟩ = a|⟨a|ψ⟩|². The squared overlap is the Born probability from Lesson 13.

Measure the spread around the mean

In the opening distribution, +1 is 1.28 above the mean and −1 is 0.72 below it. The probability-weighted signed deviations cancel. Squaring the deviations lets both distances contribute positively to the spread.

(9/25)(1.28) + (16/25)(−0.72) = 0

The variance is the probability-weighted average of the squared deviations. Its square root is the standard deviation, written ΔA. It describes the spread of repeated readings in the chosen preparation.

(ΔZ)² = (9/25)(1.28)² + (16/25)(−0.72)² = 576/625
ΔZ = √(576/625) = 24/25 = 0.96

The dashed span marks mean ± one standard deviation. In this example it runs from −1.24 to 0.68, so the possible reading +1 lies outside it. The span illustrates a distance scale for the distribution.

Derive the second-moment formula

Write μ = ⟨A⟩ for the mean. The identity operator I leaves each ket unchanged. Subtracting μI from A shifts each eigenvalue to its deviation a − μ.

(ΔA)² = Σa P(a)(a − μ)² = ⟨(A − μI)²⟩ = ⟨A²⟩ − μ²

Expanding the square gives ⟨A²⟩ − 2μ⟨A⟩ + μ²⟨I⟩. Normalization makes ⟨I⟩ = 1, leaving ⟨A²⟩ − μ². Here ⟨A²⟩ averages the squares of the readings. μ² squares the average reading.

Every Pauli observable has A² = I, so ⟨A²⟩ = 1. For the opening Z setup, the shortcut gives 1 − (−7/25)² = 1 − 49/625 = 576/625.

Keep the preparation and change the observable

Choose |0⟩ and measure Z. Every simulated reading is +1. The mean is +1 and the spread is zero. Keep |0⟩ selected and switch to X. Both readings now have probability 1/2, the mean is zero, and the spread is one.

Quantum uncertainty here describes the distribution for a particular observable and state. Even with an ideal instrument and a known pure preparation, the spread can be positive.

The sample mean uses the observed counts. If n+ and n− count the two outcomes and N is their total, then ā = (n+ − n−)/N when N is positive. A fair X sample with five of each sign has mean zero. If the next reading is +1, the mean moves to 1/11, farther from the theoretical zero. Larger samples generally improve estimates across repeated experiments. The preparation’s intrinsic ΔA stays fixed.

Compare the spreads of two observables

The Robertson relation gives a lower bound on the product of two spreads. Prepare two groups of systems in the same state. Measure A on one group and B on the other. Each spread describes its own distribution.

ΔA · ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A, B]⟩|
[A, B] = AB − BA

The bracket [A, B] is the commutator. It subtracts the two products formed in opposite orders. For X and Y, [X, Y] = 2iZ, where i² = −1. The factor ½ and the magnitude of 2i give the lower bound |⟨Z⟩|.
