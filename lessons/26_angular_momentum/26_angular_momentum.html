Show Me Why

j · Total quantum number

j sets the magnitude through ℏ√[j(j+1)] and the number of projections through 2j+1. Change it in the State Deck.

m · Projection label

m gives the component eigenvalue in units of ℏ along the chosen quantization axis. Compare the projection readout and ladder rungs.

J² · Magnitude squared

The sum of the squares of the three component operators. It has eigenvalue ℏ²j(j+1) throughout a fixed-j multiplet.

Jz · z component

Jz measures along the fixed z reference. A tilted detector instead measures the component along its own axis. Look for the +z reference line behind both arrows.

ℏ · Reduced Planck constant

ℏ = h/(2π) sets the unit of angular momentum. Component readings use ℏ and squared magnitude uses ℏ².

[A,B] · Commutator

[A,B] = AB − BA compares the two orders of operator multiplication. See the axis choices and the middle selection on the measurement track.

J± · Ladder operators

J₊ and J₋ change the projection label by one within a fixed j. The coefficient determines the norm of the output.

1. Separate the total from a projection

J² = Jₓ² + Jᵧ² + Jz²
Jn = nₓJₓ + nᵧJᵧ + nzJz, with |n| = 1

The component operators obey [Jₓ,Jᵧ] = iℏJz, [Jᵧ,Jz] = iℏJₓ, and [Jz,Jₓ] = iℏJᵧ. Meanwhile, [J²,Jn] = 0 for every axis. For example, using [A²,B] = A[A,B] + [A,B]A, the terms from Jₓ² and Jᵧ² in [J²,Jz] are equal and opposite. The other axes follow by cycling the labels.

Commuting J² and Jz have simultaneous eigenstates. In |1,0⟩ along z, Jz is exactly zero and J² is exactly 2ℏ². Here the transverse components have spread ℏ. The j = 0 state is a special case with all three components zero.

2. Count the allowed values

J²|j,m⟩ = ℏ²j(j+1)|j,m⟩
Jz|j,m⟩ = ℏm|j,m⟩

The magnitude is ℏ√[j(j+1)]. A chosen component has 2j+1 possible outcomes, with m = −j, −j+1, …, j. General j can be zero, a positive integer, or a positive half-integer. For spin-½, the magnitude is √3 ℏ/2 and the component readings are ±ℏ/2. These readouts specify a total and one projection.

3. Rotate the spin-½ detector and square the overlap

In the x–z plane, a detector whose positive axis is tilted by β from +z has a positive eigenstate |+n⟩ = cos(β/2)|+z⟩ + sin(β/2)|−z⟩. Its orthogonal negative eigenstate may be chosen as |−n⟩ = −sin(β/2)|+z⟩ + cos(β/2)|−z⟩.

Taking overlaps with |+z⟩ gives amplitudes cos(β/2) and −sin(β/2). Squaring their magnitudes gives P(+) = cos²(β/2) = (1+cos β)/2 and P(−) = sin²(β/2). For β = 60°, the positive amplitude is √3/2, and the probabilities are 75% and 25%.

P(r | s,u,v) = [1 + r s (u·v)]/2

The general rule uses preparation sign s on unit axis u, detector outcome sign r on unit axis v, and the dot product of the two directions. Reversing the source sign swaps the channel probabilities while leaving the axes as drawn.

4. Follow a selected intermediate branch

From positive on +z, a direct measurement on +z is certain to give +ℏ/2. Insert a measurement on +x and pass only its positive output. Half pass, and the survivors reach the final detector in the positive eigenstate on +x.

P(pass and final r) = P(pass) P(final r | pass)

Each final z sign is 50% of those passed. Each is (1/2)(1/2) = 25% of those sent, and the other 50% of source particles stop at the middle selector. The sequence demonstrates the consequences of choosing different axes. The commutator identity follows from the operator algebra.

5. Derive the ladder coefficient

Define J± = Jₓ ± iJᵧ. Using the cyclic commutators gives [Jz,J±] = ±ℏJ±. Acting on |j,m⟩ gives Jz(J±|j,m⟩) = ℏ(m±1)J±|j,m⟩. Since J² commutes with J±, any nonzero result stays in the same j multiplet.

Because J±† = J∓, the squared norm is ⟨j,m|J∓J±|j,m⟩. Expanding the product gives J∓J± = J² − Jz² ∓ ℏJz. Substituting the eigenvalues yields

‖J±|j,m⟩‖² = ℏ²[j(j+1) − m(m±1)]
J±|j,m⟩ = ℏ√[j(j+1) − m(m±1)] |j,m±1⟩

The zero at the top occurs at m = j for J₊, and the zero at the bottom occurs at m = −j for J₋. Since a ladder from −j to j takes steps of one, 2j is an integer. Here the rungs encode projections at fixed j. In Lesson 25, the rungs were oscillator energies. This algebra by itself gives no energy change or interaction that could drive a transition.

6. Read an orbital angular distribution

Orbital angular momentum L = r × p concerns spatial coordinates. Intrinsic spin S has its own angular momentum operators. For orbital motion, ℓ = 0,1,2,… and integer m runs from −ℓ to +ℓ. The eigenvalues are ℏ²ℓ(ℓ+1) for L² and ℏm for Lz.

Yℓm(θ,φ) = √[(2ℓ+1)(ℓ−m)!/(4π(ℓ+m)!)] Pℓm(cos θ)eimφ   (m ≥ 0)

Here Pℓm includes the Condon–Shortley factor (−1)m, and Yℓ−m = (−1)m(Yℓm)*. Write θ from +z and φ around z. Since dΩ = sin θ dθ dφ, the total density integrates to one with that measure. The phase can vary with φ while the density stays independent of it.

For ℓ = 0 the density is 1/(4π). For ℓ = 1, m = 0 it is 3cos²θ/(4π), which vanishes at the equator. For either m = ±1 it is 3sin²θ/(8π), which vanishes at the poles. The real pₓ and pᵧ angular shapes are linear combinations of the two complex m = ±1 states.

Revisit the opening experiment. The source remained positive on +z as the rotated detector changed the measured component. In the sequential experiment, selecting the middle outcome changed which state arrived at the final detector.
