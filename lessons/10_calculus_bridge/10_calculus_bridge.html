How fast is the curve changing?

Start on the parabola at x₀ = 2. Predict the slope a line would have if it matched the curve’s local direction there. Try h = +0.5 and h = −0.5. Then repeat with +0.1 and −0.1. The secants turn toward the same slope as Q approaches P.

A secant’s slope gives the average rate between two points. When the quotient approaches a single number as the second point approaches the first from either side, that number is the derivative. It gives the instantaneous rate at the base point. For position in meters plotted against time in seconds, the rate is in meters per second.

Derive the slope at x₀ = 2

[(2 + h)² − 4] / h = (4h + h²) / h = 4 + h, for h ≠ 0.

The four steps +0.5, −0.5, +0.1, and −0.1 give slopes 4.5, 3.5, 4.1, and 3.9. Because the difference from 4 equals h, taking a sufficiently small nonzero step makes the quotient as close to 4 as you want. The graph shows a few examples. The algebra establishes the limit for arbitrarily small steps.

f′(x₀) = limh → 0 [f(x₀ + h) − f(x₀)] / h, when the limit exists.

x0 is the fixed input and h is the signed run. The numerator is the signed rise. Cancellation is valid at every nonzero step here, so the quotient never needs to be evaluated at h = 0.

Try the absolute-value preset at 0. The quotients are −1 to the left and +1 to the right, however small the steps get. The slopes disagree, so there is no two-sided derivative at the corner.

How much has accumulated?

Choose Accumulation and set the parabola from 0 to 2. Predict how a left sample, a right sample, and a midpoint sample will compare with the exact area. Switch between the three rules with four rectangles, then increase the count and compare the readings.

Each rectangle uses one sample height to approximate a whole slice. With velocity on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis, a velocity in meters per second times an interval in seconds approximates displacement in meters. Negative velocities can cancel positive contributions.

Check the four-rectangle sums and the sign convention

Δx = (b − a) / n
Finite sum = Σk=1n f(xk*) Δx

a starts the interval, b ends it, and n counts equal subintervals. The marked sample xk* is an endpoint or the midpoint of subinterval k. Its height times the signed width gives that rectangle’s contribution.

  • Left samples give 1.75. On this increasing curve, each left height is the smallest in its slice.
  • Right samples give 3.75. Each right height is the largest in its slice.
  • Midpoints give 2.625. Here the upward curvature makes the slice’s average height larger than its midpoint height.
  • The exact integral is 8/3. Refining the partition makes these sums approach the integral for continuous functions. A particular increase in n need not improve every approximation.

Try signed cancellation with f(x) = x from −1 to 1. The two triangular regions contribute −1/2 and +1/2. Their net integral is 0, and their total geometric area is 1.

When a = b, every rectangle has width zero and the integral is zero. Reversing the bounds changes the sign of Δx and the exact integral. The left and right rules still sample the spatially left and right edges, so for the same subdivision their finite sums also change sign.

The height at the moving edge sets the rate

Choose Moving edge at 2. Before changing δ, predict whether the average height across the strip will approach the height at b as the strip gets narrower. Try a small positive change, then a small negative one. The change in accumulation divided by the signed change in the bound measures the strip’s average height.

Connect slopes and integrals through the Fundamental Theorem

A(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt

The starting bound a stays fixed. The running input x sets the end bound. Inside the integral, t is the integration variable that runs along the interval.

A(x + δ) − A(x) = ∫xx + δ f(t) dt

Dividing by nonzero δ gives the average height between the two bounds. The signs of the oriented integral and δ account for a step in either direction. By continuity, the heights throughout a sufficiently narrow strip are close to f(x), so their average approaches that height. Taking the limit gives A′(x) = f(x).

If F is any antiderivative with F′ = f on the interval, then A and F have the same derivative. Their difference is constant, and A(a) = 0 fixes that constant.

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

For f(t) = t², this gives A(x) = x³/3 from a = 0. At x = 2, A(2) = 8/3 is the accumulated value, and A′(2) = 4 is its instantaneous growth per unit advance of the edge. Explain which quantity you would use to estimate the effect of moving that edge by 0.01.

Where calculus returns

Derivatives will describe how quantities change with position or time. Integrating a probability density over a position interval will give the probability of a result in that interval. This lesson gives you the tools to distinguish a local rate, a density at a point, and an accumulated value over a range.

    Case 1 · The flat cubic✓ Complete

    Find f′(2) for f(x) = x³.

    1. [f(2 + h) − f(2)] / h = [(2 + h)³ − 8] / h.
    2. Set h = 0 in the quotient: (8 − 8) / 0 = 0.

    2. The order of the bounds does not matter, so ∫ from 3 to 1 of 2x dx = ∫ from 1 to 3 of 2x dx.
    3. ∫ from 1 to 3 of 2x dx = F(3) − F(1) = 9 − 1 = 8.

    Case 3 · The vanishing region✓ Complete

    Find the total area between f(x) = x − 2 and the x-axis on [0, 4].

    1. An antiderivative is F(x) = x²/2 − 2x.
    2. F(4) − F(0) = (8 − 8) − 0 = 0.
    3. So the total area between the graph and the axis is 0.
    4. The region has no size.
    Case 4 · The generous evaluation✓ Complete

    Evaluate ∫ from 1 to 2 of 3x² dx.

    1. An antiderivative of 3x² is F(x) = x³.
    2. F(2) = 8 and F(1) = 1.
    3. ∫ from 1 to 2 of 3x² dx = F(2) + F(1) = 9.
    4. So the integral is 9.
    Case 5 · The agreeable corner✓ Complete

    f(x) = 2x for x < 1 and x + 1 for x ≥ 1. Does f′(1) exist?

    1. f(1) = 1 + 1 = 2.
    2. Right side, h > 0: [(1 + h) + 1 − 2] / h = 1.
    3. Left side, h < 0: [(1 + h) + 1 − 2] / h = 1.
    4. Both sides give 1, so f′(1) = 1.
    Show the math

    Watch for five classic slips. You can’t substitute h = 0 into a difference quotient, so simplify first, then let h → 0. Reversing the bounds flips the sign: ∫ from b to a = −∫ from a to b. Total area adds the sizes of the pieces, while the integral keeps their signs. The theorem says F(b) − F(a), with a minus sign. A one-sided quotient must use the formula that holds on that side.

    d/dx e^(kx) = k·e^(kx), so the slope is always k times the height. d/dx sin x = cos x, and d/dx cos x = −sin x: each slope curve is the original shifted a quarter period. Since e^(ikx) = cos kx + i sin kx, its derivative is −k sin kx + ik cos kx = ik·e^(ikx). Multiplying by i rotates by 90°, so the velocity is perpendicular to the position and has length |k|. That is the steady phase rotation from Lesson 6.
