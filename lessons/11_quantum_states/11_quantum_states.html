Lesson 11 · Pure states and amplitudes

Quantum States

A state builder contains the numbers 3 and 4i. How can you turn them into the amplitudes of a normalized quantum state while preserving their relative sizes and complex phases?

A quantum state is a mathematical description of a preparation that we use to predict measurement outcomes. A pure state can be represented by a vector of norm 1. In this lesson the vector has two complex amplitudes, one for each member of a chosen basis.

What the two amplitudes describe

A ket is the notation we use for a state vector. In an ideal two-level system, the two basis kets |0⟩ and |1⟩ span the space, so a coefficient of each is enough to describe any vector in it. The labels 0 and 1 are names for the basis states.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

As in Lesson 8, the expansion and the ordered coefficient column describe the same vector. The basis is orthonormal. Each basis ket has norm 1, and their inner product is zero. The two lab arrows show α and β in their own complex planes. Their directions encode complex phases.

Use the inner product to find the norm

For a coefficient z = a + bi with real parts a and b, its complex conjugate z* is a − bi. Multiplying the two gives the squared magnitude.

|z|² = z*z = (a − bi)(a + bi) = a² + b²

The bra ⟨ψ| is the conjugate transpose of the ket |ψ⟩. In the chosen basis it is the row (α*, β*). Expanding the inner product of the bra and ket gives four terms.

⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = α*α⟨0|0⟩ + α*β⟨0|1⟩ + β*α⟨1|0⟩ + β*β⟨1|1⟩

The cross terms vanish because ⟨0|1⟩ and ⟨1|0⟩ are zero. Each basis ket’s inner product with itself is 1.

‖ψ‖² = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = α*α + β*β = |α|² + |β|²

The squared norm adds the two squared magnitudes. The norm ‖ψ‖ is the nonnegative square root of that sum. For a normalized representative, both the norm and the squared norm are 1.

Normalize without turning the arrows

Start with the opening candidate (3, 4i)ᵀ. Both arrows need to shrink by the same positive factor. That preserves their relative sizes and the angles of both nonzero coefficients. The superscript T indicates a column written as a pair.

Work through the opening solution
  1. Find the squared norm. The lengths are 3 and 4, so |3|² + |4i|² = 9 + 16 = 25.
  2. Take the square root. The norm is √25 = 5.
  3. Divide both coefficients by 5. The normalized column is (3/5, 4i/5)ᵀ.
  4. Check the result. The new squared norm is 9/25 + 16/25 = 1.

Dividing both coefficients by 25 would give a squared norm of 25/25² = 1/25. Scaling a vector by 1/5 scales its norm by 1/5 and its squared norm by 1/25.

The zero pair has norm 0. Every finite scaling of it still gives zero, so it cannot be normalized. An individual zero coefficient is allowed when the other coefficient gives the vector norm 1, as in |0⟩ or |1⟩. A zero coefficient has no defined phase.

A preview of the Born rule

For a normalized state measured in this chosen basis, the Born rule gives the probabilities by squaring the magnitudes of the two amplitudes. The normalized opening example gives 9/25 for outcome 0 and 16/25 for outcome 1. Lesson 13 will develop this rule.

In the lab, switch between Real β and Imaginary β. The α arrow stays at 3/5. The β arrow turns by 90° from the positive real axis to the positive imaginary axis and keeps its magnitude 4/5. The probabilities in this basis stay the same. Lesson 12 asks what that change in relative phase can affect.

          Show the math

          A point at angle θ from the top and longitude φ has coordinates (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ). The latitude comes from magnitudes alone: |α| = cos(θ/2) and |β| = sin(θ/2), so |α|² + |β|² = 1 for every point. Opposite points, such as |0⟩ and |1⟩ or |+⟩ and |−⟩, are orthogonal states.
