Complex Numbers and Phase

Measure the length and choose the angle

The axis projections form a right triangle with leg lengths |a| and |b|. Pythagoras gives the magnitude r, which is the distance from the origin.

r = |z| = √(a² + b²)

For 3 + 4i, the squared length is 9 + 16 = 25, so the length is 5 units. For a nonzero point, atan2(b, a) uses both signed components to choose the quadrant. The ratio b/a alone would give the same value for (3, 4) and (−3, −4), even though their arrows point in opposite directions.

Add or subtract 360° and you describe the same endpoint. At the origin, r is zero and the argument is undefined because the arrow has no direction.

Build rectangular form from polar form

For a nonzero arrow of length r, cosine gives the signed horizontal component per unit length, and sine gives the signed vertical component per unit length. Multiply each by r.

a = r cos θ
b = r sin θ
z = r(cos θ + i sin θ)

Try r = 5 and θ = 90° in the lab, then change only the radius to 2. The tip moves from 5i to 2i along the same direction. Hold the radius at 2 and change the angle to 180° to reach −2 without changing the length.

One turn around the unit circle

Set the magnitude to 1. As θ increases, cos θ + i sin θ traces the unit circle counterclockwise from 1. Euler’s formula identifies that point with a complex exponential. Use radians for θ in the identity.

eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ

Optional derivation from the exponential series

The exponential series gives eiθ = 1 + iθ + (iθ)²/2! + (iθ)³/3! + ⋯. The factorial 3! means 3 × 2 × 1, and the powers of i cycle through 1, i, −1, −i.

The even powers give 1 − θ²/2! + θ⁴/4! − ⋯, which is the cosine series. The odd powers give i(θ − θ³/3! + θ⁵/5! − ⋯), which is i times the sine series. Adding those two parts gives Euler’s formula.

Check the opening turn with algebra

A quarter-turn counterclockwise takes the positive real direction to the positive imaginary direction, and the positive imaginary direction to the negative real direction. Multiplication by i does exactly that.

i(a + bi) = ai + bi² = −b + ai

For 3 + 4i, the result is −4 + 3i, or the endpoint (−4, 3). The squared magnitudes on either side are 3² + 4² and (−4)² + 3². Both equal 25, so the magnitude stays 5.

Four quarter-turns make one full turn. The coordinates return to their starting values. The principal angle may wrap during the turn because the readout reports it in a fixed interval.

Why phase comes back in quantum mechanics

Quantum amplitudes can be complex. When amplitudes combine, their relative phase can affect interference. Two arrows can keep their individual lengths while turning relative to one another, changing the length of their sum.

The arrow in this lab represents one mathematical number. Later lessons will explain how complex numbers enter quantum states and how the Born rule relates amplitudes to probabilities. Next, we’ll use vectors and learn how an inner product compares them.
