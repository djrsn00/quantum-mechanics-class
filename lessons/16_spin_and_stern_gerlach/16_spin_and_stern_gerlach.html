Spin and Stern–Gerlach

A source prepares every atom in |+z⟩, so a Z analyzer always gives +ℏ/2. How can inserting an X analyzer and passing only its positive channel make a later Z analyzer give some negative readings?

Follow one atom from the source to its endpoint. Then compare the predictions with a simulated beam.

Stern–Gerlach lab

Spin is intrinsic angular momentum. In this model, a beam of neutral atoms has an effective spin-½ degree of freedom. A magnetic-field gradient exerts a force that depends on the magnetic moment along the analyzer’s axis. The two possible readings separate into two beam channels. A uniform field alone gives no such spatial splitting.

The axis arrow defines the positive direction. A detector at the end of a channel registers the spin component along that axis as +ℏ/2 or −ℏ/2. The lines below show the apparatus’s output channels. Each simulated atom follows the channel sampled at each active stage.

A different axis asks a different question

The analyzer chooses which component of spin to measure. Rotating it changes the measurement basis while the source keeps preparing the same state. In the Z basis, |ψ⟩ = α|+z⟩ + β|−z⟩. The complex coefficients α and β satisfy |α|² + |β|² = 1. The probabilities of the positive and negative Z channels are |α|² and |β|².

See the operator and derive the half-angle rule

Sn = (ℏ/2) n · σ

ℏ is the reduced Planck constant, h/(2π), and has units of angular momentum. The unit vector n points along the analyzer’s positive axis. The dot product n · σ combines the dimensionless Pauli matrices X, Y and Z using the components of n. Its eigenvalues are +1 and −1, so Sn has readings +ℏ/2 and −ℏ/2.

In the xz-plane, the dial angle η is measured from +z toward +x. The positive eigenstate of this analyzer is

|+n⟩ = cos(η/2)|+z⟩ + sin(η/2)|−z⟩.

For |+z⟩ input, the overlap ⟨+n|+z⟩ is cos(η/2) because |+z⟩ and |−z⟩ are orthonormal. Squaring its magnitude gives the positive-channel probability. The negative channel gets the remainder.

P(+) = cos²(θ/2) = (1 + s · n)/2
P(−) = sin²(θ/2) = (1 − s · n)/2

Here s is the prepared positive spin direction, and θ is the separation angle between s and n. The dot product is cos θ. With |+z⟩, θ = η in this lab. With |−z⟩, s points along −z, so θ = 180° − η. For example, |+z⟩ at η = 60° gives P(+) = cos²(30°) = 3/4.

The selector prepares the beam that reaches B

A splits the beam. The selector blocks one branch and lets the other continue without an absorbing detector in its path. An atom at B is in A’s selected eigenstate under this ideal model. With B aligned to A and no intervening spin evolution, B repeats the selected sign. When B’s axis changes, calculate the probabilities using the selected state as the new preparation.

Work through the opening puzzle

Direct Z. The |+z⟩ source has probability 1 at the positive Z detector. No atoms reach its negative detector in this ideal setup.

X+ then Z. A’s positive X channel receives half of the |+z⟩ source beam. The selector passes that half to B in |+x⟩. Z is perpendicular to X, so half of those passed atoms reach B’s negative detector. Multiplying 1/2 × 1/2 gives 1/4 of all atoms sent. The other three quarters are blocked at A (1/2) or reach B+ (1/4).

X− then X. A passes its negative X channel, again half of the source beam. B uses the same positive axis as A, so every atom that reaches B goes to B−. That is 100% of the passed atoms and 50% of all atoms sent.

Calculate a sequential probability

Let q be +1 for a positive selection at A and −1 for a negative selection. The passed state points along q nA, where nA is A’s positive axis. The dot product with B’s positive axis nB gives the cosine of the new separation angle.

PB(+ | A = q) = (1 + q nA · nB)/2

The vertical bar means “given that.” The probability of B− in the passed beam is the complement. To get either B probability per source atom, multiply its conditional probability by PA(q), the probability of reaching the passed branch at A. If PA(q) is zero, B receives no beam.

Classical vs Quantum Screen

An oven sends silver atoms through the magnet onto a plate. If each atom were a tiny classical spinning magnet, its moment could point any way, and its deflection would be proportional to its z component μ_z = μ cos α. Pick a model and send atoms. Each model keeps its own plate.

History: Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach ran this experiment in Frankfurt in 1922. Their glass plate showed the silver deposit split in two, with a gap where the classical picture predicts the densest part of a band. They read it as Bohr–Sommerfeld “space quantization”. Electron spin was proposed only in 1925, and silver’s moment comes from its single unpaired outer electron.

Show the math

Classical: the deflection is proportional to μ cos α. For moments pointing every way equally often, the share of directions with cos α in a range is proportional to the length of that range, so cos α is uniform on [−1, 1]. The middle half, |cos α| < 1/2, has probability 1/2.

Quantum: an S_z analyzer has two readings, +ℏ/2 and −ℏ/2, so atoms land only at the two largest deflections. An oven’s random spins put half in each spot. The spots have some width because the beam itself has width.

Gradient and Deflection

Look down the beam. The magnet’s axis u points from the beam toward the end marked +u. Its field points along u, and the pointed pole makes the field stronger on that side. The force on an atom is F = μ_u ∂B_u/∂u along u. A silver atom’s magnetic moment points opposite its spin, so an atom with S_u = +ℏ/2 has μ_u < 0. Change the gradient and roll the magnet, then decide where each channel goes. Tick the box to check your reasoning on the diagram.

Show the math

The energy of a moment in a field is U = −μ·B, so the force is F = −∇U = ∇(μ·B). Along the magnet’s axis, F_u = μ_u ∂B_u/∂u. For silver, μ = −γS with γ > 0, so S_u = +ℏ/2 gives μ_u < 0 and the force points toward the weaker field.

Reversing the gradient flips the sign of ∂B_u/∂u, so the channels swap places. Rolling the magnet by 180° reverses both B and the direction of u, which leaves ∂B_z/∂z unchanged: the atoms land where they did, and only the names of the channels swap. A uniform field has ∂B/∂u = 0, so there is no split.

    Recombine the Beams

    A Z magnet splits the beam into a +z path and a −z path. A second magnet with the opposite gradient (Z⁻¹) brings them back together before a final analyzer F along n. If nothing records which path an atom took, the original state comes back. Block a path or tag it with a which-path marker and it does not.

    Idealized thought experiment. Recombining the two paths so precisely that nothing, not even the atom’s position or momentum, records the path is extremely hard to do in practice. Here the magnets are perfect.

    Show the math

    Split: |ψ⟩ = α|+z⟩ + β|−z⟩, with each term sent along its own path. Recombined with no record, the terms rejoin with their relative phase, so the state is α|+z⟩ + β|−z⟩ again. F then gives P(+) = (1 + s·n)/2 with the original s.

    Blocking the −z path keeps only α|+z⟩: a share |α|² survives, in |+z⟩. A which-path record leaves each atom in |+z⟩ or |−z⟩ with probabilities |α|² and |β|², which is the same as a Z measurement, so P(F+) = |α|²(1 + n_z)/2 + |β|²(1 − n_z)/2.

    Magnitude vs Component

    For spin s = 1/2, S² = S_x² + S_y² + S_z² has the value s(s + 1)ℏ² = (3/4)ℏ² on every state, so the spin’s magnitude is √(s(s + 1))ℏ = (√3/2)ℏ. A component reads only ±ℏ/2, which is smaller. The cone is the old vector-model picture of a state with one definite component: the arrow’s azimuth is not a real, measurable angle. Choose which component is definite.

    [S_x, S_y] = (ℏ/2)²[X, Y] = (ℏ²/4)(2iZ) = iℏS_z, and cyclically. Because the components don’t commute, no state is an eigenket of two of them, so at most one component can have a definite value.

    Error Detective

    Each case file holds a worked solution with exactly one planted mistake. Find the first line that goes wrong, and name the mistake. The diagram shows what the solution claims next to what is actually true.

    Error Detective controls

    Case 1 · the 60° analyzer✓ Complete

    |+z⟩ enters an analyzer at η = 60°. Find P(+).

    1. The separation angle is θ = 60°.
    2. P(+) = cos²θ = cos²60° = 1/4.
    3. So P(−) = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4.
    4. Most atoms read −ℏ/2.
    Case 2 · the share at B−✓ Complete

    |+z⟩ → A along x, passing A+ → B along z. What share of all atoms sent reaches B−?

    1. P_A(+) = 1/2.
    2. The passed atoms are in |+x⟩, so P_B(− | A = +) = 1/2.
    3. So B− receives 1/2 of all atoms sent.
    4. B+ receives the other 1/2.
    Case 3 · three analyzers✓ Complete

    |+z⟩ → A along x passing + → B along z passing − → C along x. What share of all atoms sent reaches C+?

    1. P_A(+) = 1/2, and the passed atoms are in |+x⟩.
    2. P_B(− | A = +) = 1/2, and the passed atoms are in |−z⟩.
    3. P_C(+ | B = −) = 1/2.
    4. Share at C+ = P_B(− | A = +) × P_C(+ | B = −) = 1/4.
    Case 4 · what the selector does✓ Complete

    |+z⟩ → A along x, with the selector passing A+ → B along x. Find P_B(+ | A = +).

    1. P_A(+) = 1/2.
    2. The selector is a detector that absorbs each atom it registers in A+.
    3. So no atoms reach B, and P_B(+ | A = +) is undefined.
    4. Only the A− atoms could ever reach B.
    Case 5 · where |+z⟩ points✓ Complete

    A source prepares |+z⟩. Describe its spin.

    1. An S_z analyzer gives +ℏ/2 with certainty.
    2. So the spin vector points exactly along +z, with length ℏ/2.
    3. Its x component is therefore exactly 0.
    4. So an S_x analyzer reads 0 on every atom.
    Show the math

    The planted mistakes: the spin rule is P(+) = cos²(θ/2), not cos²θ. A share of all atoms sent includes every factor from the source onward, so blocked atoms count, and P_A(q) multiplies the conditionals. A selector blocks one channel and lets the other pass untouched. A definite S_z doesn’t make the spin point along z: |S| = (√3/2)ℏ > ℏ/2, and S_x stays ±ℏ/2.

    Read the oscillation✓ Complete

    Input |+x⟩, analyzer along +x. Find P(+) at t = T/3.

    Along the field✓ Complete

    Input |+z⟩ with the analyzer along +z. What does the delay do?

    Show the math

    The spin direction after a delay t is s(t) = (cos 2πt/T, sin 2πt/T, 0) for a |+x⟩ start. An analyzer along n gives P(+) = (1 + s(t)·n)/2, so along +x, P(+) = cos²(πt/T).

    This is only the pattern. Why the spin turns, and how the field sets T, is time evolution, which Lesson 17 develops.

    Show the math

    Each magnet sends a beam in state s into + with probability (1 + s·n)/2, leaving in |+n⟩, and into − with the rest, leaving in |−n⟩. A spot’s share is the product of its path’s probabilities, and the shares of all spots add to 1.

    A magnet’s gradient is across the beam, so rolling covers every axis perpendicular to +y. For a source along +y, s·n = 0 for every roll.

    Why spin-½ is special✓ Complete

    Turn the analyzer to where P(0) is largest. Give that largest value and say what it shows.

    Show the math

    For |m = +1⟩ along z and an analyzer at θ: P(+ℏ) = ((1 + cos θ)/2)², P(0) = sin²θ/2 and P(−ℏ) = ((1 − cos θ)/2)². They add to 1.

    For spin-½, every state is |+n⟩ for some direction n. For spin-1 that fails: P(0) for this state is at most 1/2. Lesson 26 develops general angular momentum.
