Read a vector and measure its magnitude

A component tells you how far and in which signed direction to step along one axis. In the Components overlay, follow x, y, then z to reach the tip. The arrow from the origin records the resulting direction and distance. That distance is the vector’s magnitude.

First find the diagonal of the right triangle in the XY plane. Its squared length is ax² + ay². Use that diagonal and the perpendicular z leg in a second right triangle.

‖a‖ = √(ax² + ay² + az²)

For a = (1, 1, 1), the squared length is 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 square units, so the magnitude is √3 units. The axes determine the component description. Moving the camera simply gives you another view of those same axes and components.

Add two arrows tip to tail

Load the opening pair and choose Addition. Slide a copy of b so its tail sits at the tip of a. The drawing moves, and its steps along each axis stay the same. The resultant a + b runs from the origin to the tip of that translated copy.

Work through the opening sum

Along x the two steps are 1 and 1. Along y they are also 1 and 1. Along z they are 1 and −2. Add each pair separately.

a + b = (1 + 1, 1 + 1, 1 − 2) = (2, 2, −1)

Use the dot product to settle the puzzle

The real Euclidean inner product here is the dot product. It combines two vectors into a single number, called a scalar. Multiply components on the same axis and add those three products. For nonzero vectors, the dot product also equals the product of the magnitudes times the cosine of their angle.

a · b = axbx + ayby + azbz
a · b = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos θ

Here θ is the angle between the full arrows, from 0 to π radians. Dividing by ‖a‖ ‖b‖ gives cos θ for two nonzero vectors. The raw dot product depends on lengths and alignment. The normalized value isolates alignment.

Check the opening puzzle by hand

For (1, 1, 1) and (1, 1, −2), the component products are +1, +1, and −2. They add to zero. Both vectors are nonzero, so their cosine is zero and their angle is 90°. Their identical XY shadows hide the depth terms that make this possible.

See why the cosine formula works

The squared distance between the tips is ‖a − b‖². Expanding the squared differences of the coordinates gives ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2(a · b). The law of cosines gives the same distance as ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2‖a‖ ‖b‖ cos θ. Equating the two expressions gives the cosine formula. Parallel and opposite directions use the limiting angles 0 and π.

For nonzero vectors, positive cosine means an acute angle or the parallel endpoint 0°. Negative cosine means an obtuse angle or the opposite endpoint 180°. The Parallel and Opposite presets show those endpoints.

Project b onto the direction of a

Choose Projection · b onto a. Imagine a line through the origin in the direction of a. Drop a perpendicular guide from the tip of b to that line. The landing point is the tip of the vector projection. This operation uses all three components and differs from taking an XY shadow.

Try the −3 depth case from the sign experiment. The signed component is negative, so the landing point lies on the ray opposite a.

Calculate the signed component and the projected arrow

For nonzero a, divide the dot product by the magnitude of a to get the signed scalar component of b along a. It is a number in units of length. Multiply it by the unit direction a/‖a‖ to get the vector projection.

Signed component = (a · b) / ‖a‖
proja b = [(a · b) / (a · a)] a

In the −3 depth case the dot product is −1 and ‖a‖ = √3. The scalar component is −1/√3 units, and the vector projection is (−⅓, −⅓, −⅓).

Check what zero changes

The zero vector has magnitude zero, no direction, and dot product zero with every vector. If either vector is zero, the angle is undefined. A zero dot product establishes a right angle only when both vectors are nonzero.

If a = (0, 0, 0), projection onto its direction is undefined. If b = (0, 0, 0) and a is nonzero, the projection is the zero vector. Try both zero presets and compare the readouts.

From arrows to state spaces

This lab used ordinary real vectors in three dimensions. In later lessons, quantum states can live in abstract complex vector spaces. Length and orthogonality still matter there. An inner product gives us a way to define and calculate both, once we know the rules for that space.

Next, we’ll look at how the same vector can have different components when we describe it using a different basis.
