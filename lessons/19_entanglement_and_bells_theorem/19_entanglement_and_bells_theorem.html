Alice and Bob can agree on a plan before a round. Once they receive their separate questions, they cannot communicate. Can they win more than three quarters of the rounds?

The CHSH game

Alice receives input bit x and answers with output bit a. Bob receives y and answers with b. The game draws the two inputs independently, with 0 and 1 equally likely for each player. The referee compares the answers after both players respond.

Win by matching answers on inputs 00, 01, and 10. Win by giving different answers on input 11. In symbols, a XOR b must equal x AND y. XOR is 0 for matching bits and 1 for different bits.

Start with the all-zero classical plan or change a reply. Play one round and check which rule decided it. Then play 100 rounds and compare with a run using the quantum strategy.

What changes when you choose a different basis?

Lesson 18 used the state |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, with Alice’s qubit first and Bob’s second. The two nonzero squared amplitudes sum to 1, so this state is normalized.

⟨Φ⁺|Φ⁺⟩ = |1/√2|² + |1/√2|² = 1

(P(00), P(01), P(10), P(11)) = (1/2, 0, 0, 1/2)

In the Z basis, Alice’s probability of 0 is P(00) + P(01) = 1/2. Bob’s probability of 0 is P(00) + P(10) = 1/2. Each observer therefore gets 50/50 locally, even though their paired results always match.

If |Φ⁺⟩ factored into two single-qubit kets, its amplitudes would have the form (ac, ad, bc, bd), where a and b here are the first ket’s amplitudes and c and d are the second’s. Nonzero ac and bd require all four factors to be nonzero. Then ad and bc would also be nonzero, contradicting the zero amplitudes at 01 and 10.

A source that chooses |00⟩ or |11⟩ with equal probability produces those same Z records. Try X on both qubits instead. Write |±⟩ = (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2. The pure state becomes

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|++⟩ + |−−⟩)/√2.

The pure state still gives matching results on every trial. On either component of the mixture, an X measurement gives each sign with probability 1/2 on each qubit. Each component is a product, so each X pair has probability 1/4, and so does the mixture. This comparison distinguishes the two specified preparations. Bell’s theorem asks which correlations any local hidden-variable strategy can produce.

Derive the spin-singlet predictions

The game uses a different Bell state, |Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2. The opening |Φ⁺⟩ state gives matching Z results. The singlet gives opposite results along any common spin axis. If you rotate both basis states by the same spin rotation U, the antisymmetric combination |01⟩ − |10⟩ gets multiplied by det(U), a phase of magnitude 1. It therefore keeps its opposite-sign form in the rotated basis.

Measure the oriented axis angle θ in the X–Z plane from +z toward +x. The Pauli operators σz and σx represent spin along +z and +x in units of ℏ/2. The normalized observable for θ is

M(θ) = cos θ σz + sin θ σx.

The normalized outcomes are +1 and −1. Multiply either by ℏ/2 to get the physical spin reading. Let u and v be Alice’s and Bob’s signs. Both use the same mapping from signs to game bits, +1 → 0 and −1 → 1. Thus uv = (−1)a XOR b.

Take Alice’s axis α as the reference, and let β be Bob’s axis and δ = α − β. Alice gets + with probability 1/2. Conditional on her + result, Bob’s spin is in the − state along α. In Alice’s basis, the square of the overlap between that state and Bob’s + eigenstate is sin²(δ/2). The half angle enters the spin-state overlap. δ itself is the physical angle difference.

P(++) = (1/2) sin²(δ/2) = (1 − cos δ)/4

P(−−) = P(++) = (1 − cos δ)/4

P(+−) = P(−+) = (1 + cos δ)/4

Equal signs contribute +1 to uv, and unequal signs contribute −1. The correlation E, the expected value of uv, is therefore

E(α, β) = P(equal) − P(unequal)

= (1 − cos δ)/2 − (1 + cos δ)/2 = −cos δ.

P(u, v | α, β) = [1 + uv E(α, β)]/4

To get Alice’s marginal, sum over Bob’s signs. The result is [(1 + uE) + (1 − uE)]/4 = 1/2. Summing over Alice’s signs gives the same result for Bob. Each local record remains 50/50 across the other observer’s choices, so a choice of axis cannot encode a faster-than-light message in that record.

From the CHSH bound to the scoreboard

Let Exy be the average product of the signs at input pair (x, y). The signed CHSH sum is

S = E00 + E01 + E10 − E11.

Classical local strategies

Alice and Bob may share a random variable λ before the settings are drawn. Alice’s response depends on x and λ, and Bob’s depends on y and λ. The settings are chosen independently of λ, and each observer responds using only local information and the agreed strategy. Include any private random choices among the strategy’s random variables. Fixing the variables then fixes the response table.

For one such table, let A0, A1, B0, and B1 be its four signs, each +1 or −1. Distribute the products in the CHSH sum and factor by Alice’s setting.

A0(B0 + B1) + A1(B0 − B1)

Bob’s two signs either match or differ. One bracket is zero, and the other is +2 or −2. Each table therefore gives +2 or −2. Averaging over the tables keeps |S| ≤ 2. The bound applies to models with local responses and settings independent of the strategy variables.

Expected wins

Since Exy = P(equal) − P(unequal) and those two probabilities sum to 1, the chance of equal signs is (1 + Exy)/2. The chances of winning on 00, 01, and 10 use that expression. On 11 a win requires different signs, with probability (1 − E11)/2. Each input pair has probability 1/4, so

P(win) = ¼[(1 + E00)/2 + (1 + E01)/2

    + (1 + E10)/2 + (1 − E11)/2]

= 1/2 + S/8.

Use signed S in the game formula. The classical maximum is 1/2 + 2/8 = 3/4. The opening all-zero table reaches it by winning 00, 01, and 10 and losing 11. With the chosen singlet axes, every input pair has winning probability (1 + 1/√2)/2 = cos²(π/8), so the expected score is about 85.36%.

Show the math

For the singlet, P(equal signs) = (1 − cos δ)/2 and P(unequal) = (1 + cos δ)/2, so E = P(equal) − P(unequal) = −cos δ. Only the difference δ matters, and adding 360° to either axis changes nothing.

In the model, λ is a direction spread evenly round the circle. Alice reports + when λ lies within 90° of α, and Bob reports − when λ lies within 90° of β. Their signs are equal exactly when λ lies within 90° of just one of the two axes, which happens for a fraction |δ|/180° of the circle when |δ| ≤ 180°. So E = |δ|/180° − (1 − |δ|/180°) = −1 + |δ|/90°. The two curves meet at 0°, ±90° and 180°; in between the cosine bends away from the straight lines. The largest gap, about 0.21, is near |δ| ≈ 39.5° and 140.5°, where sin δ = 2/π.

Show the math

X|0⟩ = |1⟩, X|1⟩ = |0⟩, Y|0⟩ = i|1⟩, Y|1⟩ = −i|0⟩ and Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩. A Bell state is an eigenstate of X ⊗ X, Y ⊗ Y and Z ⊗ Z, so each same-basis correlation is ±1. For example (Y ⊗ Y)|00⟩ = (i)(i)|11⟩ = −|11⟩, so EYY = −1 for |Φ⁺⟩.

Listed as (EXX, EYY, EZZ): |Φ⁺⟩ (+1, −1, +1), |Φ⁻⟩ (−1, +1, +1), |Ψ⁺⟩ (+1, +1, −1), |Ψ⁻⟩ (−1, −1, −1). Since (X ⊗ X)(Y ⊗ Y)(Z ⊗ Z) = (XYZ) ⊗ (XYZ) = (iI) ⊗ (iI) = −I, the product is −1 for every Bell state. Mixed bases such as Z on Alice and X on Bob give E = 0, and every marginal is 50/50.

      Show the math

      A table fixes four signs A₀, A₁, B₀, B₁ = ±1 (bit 0 → +1). Its CHSH sum is A₀(B₀ + B₁) + A₁(B₀ − B₁).           Alice and Bob share singlets. Alice picks her axis to send Bob one bit; Bob always measures along β = 0°.

          1. With α = 0°, δ = 0° and E = −cos 0° = −1: Bob’s sign is always opposite to Alice’s.
          2. With α = 90°, δ = 90° and E = 0: their signs are uncorrelated.
          3. So the pair statistics depend on Alice’s choice of α.
          4. Bob can therefore read Alice’s bit from his own results as soon as they arrive, faster than light.
          Case 2 · nine wins in ten✓ Complete

          A lab plays 10 CHSH rounds with an unknown device and wins 9 of them.

          1. The observed win rate is 9/10 = 90%.
          2. Any local strategy has an expected win rate of at most 3/4.
          3. 90% is above both 3/4 and the quantum value cos²(π/8) ≈ 85%.
          4. So the device must be using entanglement.
          Case 3 · a CHSH sum✓ Complete

          Find S for α₀ = 45°, α₁ = 135°, β₀ = 270°, β₁ = 180°, with q = 1/√2.

          1. E₀₀ = −cos(45° − 270°) = −cos(−225°) = +q.
          2. E₀₁ = −cos(45° − 180°) = +q, and E₁₀ = −cos(135° − 270°) = +q.
          3. E₁₁ = −cos(135° − 180°) = −cos(−45°) = −q.
          4. S = E₀₀ + E₀₁ + E₁₀ + E₁₁ = q + q + q − q = 2q = √2, so these axes don’t beat the classical bound.
          Case 4 · Alice’s own odds✓ Complete

          Singlet, α = 0°, β = 60°. Find the chance that Alice gets +1.

          1. δ = 0° − 60° = −60°, so cos δ = 1/2.
          2. P(+1, +1) = (1 − cos δ)/4 = 1/8 and P(+1, −1) = (1 + cos δ)/4 = 3/8.
          3. Alice’s +1 is the case where Bob also gets +1, so P(Alice +1) = P(+1, +1) = 1/8.
          4. So by turning β, Bob changes Alice’s local odds.
          Case 5 · a negative S✓ Complete

          Some axes give E₀₀ = E₀₁ = E₁₀ = −q and E₁₁ = +q, with q = 1/√2.

          1. S = −q − q − q − q = −4q = −2√2.
          2. |S| = 2√2 is larger than 2, the classical limit on |S|.
          3. P(win) = 1/2 + |S|/8 = 1/2 + √2/4 ≈ 85%.
          4. So this team wins about 85% of its rounds.
          Show the math

          The planted mistakes: Bob’s local record is 50/50 for every choice Alice makes, so no message travels; only the comparison of both records shows a correlation. A short run can beat its expected rate by luck: the bound limits the expected value, not 10 rounds.

          S = E₀₀ + E₀₁ + E₁₀ − E₁₁: the 11 term enters with a minus sign. Alice’s marginal sums over Bob’s results, P(Alice +1) = P(+1, +1) + P(+1, −1) = 1/2. The game’s win rate uses signed S: P(win) = 1/2 + S/8, so S = −2√2 gives about 15%, not 85%.

          Quantum Preview: EPR in Position and Momentum controls

          Elements of reality✓ Complete

          EPR’s argument rested on one criterion. Which is it?

          Predict particle 2✓ Complete

          The source fixes x₁ − x₂ = L = 3 and p₁ + p₂ = 0 (in some units). If Alice finds x₁ = 5, what is x₂? If instead she finds p₁ = +2, what is p₂?

          Does Alice’s choice reach Bob?✓ Complete

          Alice chooses to measure position or momentum. Bob only looks at his own particle’s results.

          What Lesson 20 adds✓ Complete

          Position takes a continuum of values, not two.

          Show the math

          In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen described two particles whose positions are perfectly correlated (x₁ − x₂ fixed) and whose momenta are perfectly anticorrelated (p₁ + p₂ fixed). Measuring x₁ lets you predict x₂ with certainty, and measuring p₁ lets you predict p₂, without touching particle 2. EPR argued both values must already be elements of reality, so quantum mechanics, which can’t assign both at once, would be incomplete.

          Bohm later recast the argument with two spins, the version this lesson uses, and Bell (1964) showed that local elements of reality can’t reproduce the singlet’s correlations. Lesson 20 builds the language for the original version: wavefunctions, amplitudes over a continuum of positions.

          Loophole Lab

          A Bell test only rules out local hidden variables if the experiment meets the assumptions of the derivation. Here local models exploit three gaps: discarding rounds where a detector didn’t fire, settings chosen too early or slowly, and settings the source could predict. The timeline shows how experiments closed them.

          Beat the light signal✓ Complete

          Opening this mission shows stations 400 m apart. What is the longest time from Alice’s setting choice to her finished measurement that still keeps Bob’s setting from reaching her? Use c = 299,792,458 m/s.

          Freedom of choice✓ Complete

          Suppose the source could know the settings in advance, for example because they come from a predictable list.

          Timeline✓ Complete

          Opening this mission shows the timeline. Match each year to its event.

          Show the math

          Detection: in a guessing pair the source picks a setting pair (x′, y′) at random and prepares outputs that win for it; each detector fires only if its own setting matches the guess. Coincidences then happen only when both guesses are right, and those rounds always win. The rest of the time the source uses the all-zero table, which wins 3/4. Counting only coincidences gives W = (3 − 2w)/(4 − 3w), so S = 8W − 4 looks larger than 2 for any w > 0. Each detector fires on a fraction η = 1 − w/2 of its particles. With w = 1/2, W = 4/5, S = 2.4 and η = 3/4.

          Locality: Bob’s setting choice can affect Alice only if light from it reaches her before her measurement finishes, so the time must be less than d/c. At 400 m that is 400/299,792,458 s ≈ 1.334 μs. Freedom of choice: the bound averages S over λ with weights independent of x and y. Loophole-free tests in 2015 closed detection and locality together and used fast random number generators for the settings.

          Quantum Key Distribution (E91)

          Alice and Bob use singlets to build a shared secret key. Rounds where their axes match give key bits; rounds with CHSH settings test whether the pairs are still entangled. An eavesdropper, Eve, who measures particles on the way spoils the correlations and pushes |S| toward the classical range. Bob’s first 32 key bits are shown flipped, as he uses them.

          The key rate✓ Complete

          Is someone listening?✓ Complete

          Opening this mission picks the mystery channel. Run at least 900 rounds, read |Ŝ| from your own test rounds, and decide.

          Show the math

          E91 (Ekert, 1991): Alice measures along 0°, 45° or 90° and Bob along 45°, 90° or 135°, each chosen at random. The pairs (45°, 45°) and (90°, 90°) give perfectly opposite signs, so Bob flips his bit and both hold the same key bit: 2 of 9 setting pairs. The four pairs with Alice at 90° or 0° and Bob at 45° or 135° form a CHSH test (x = 0 → 90°, x = 1 → 0°, y = 0 → 45°, y = 1 → 135°). For the singlet these give S = −2√2, so E91 checks |S|. The other three pairs are discarded.

          If Eve measures Bob’s particle along a random axis θ and resends it, the pair becomes a product: E(α, β) = −cos(α − θ)cos(β − θ), which averages to −(1/2)cos(α − β) over θ. So every correlation halves: |S| = √2 and matched axes disagree with probability 1/4. With a fraction f intercepted, |S| = 2√2(1 − f/2) and the error rate is f/4. |S| stays above 2 only while f < 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586.
