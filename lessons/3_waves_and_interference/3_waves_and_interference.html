Waves and Interference

If two waves each have an amplitude of 1 cm, can their sum have an amplitude of 2 cm, 1 cm, or zero?

Make a prediction, then change how the waves line up. For each test, compare what happens at one point with what happens across the whole string.

Meet one wave

Start with the Wave 1 snapshot in the lab. At 0 s, the crests at 0 m and 4 m are 4 m apart. The dashed horizontal line is the string’s equilibrium position. The dot at 2 m marks a point on the string, shown at a trough in this snapshot.

Amplitude
The maximum distance from equilibrium. Wave 1 starts with an amplitude of 1 cm.
Wavelength
The distance between adjacent crests at the same instant. Both waves have a wavelength of 4 m.
Frequency
Cycles per second at a fixed position. At 0.5 Hz, the point at the probe takes 2 s to complete a cycle.
Phase
Where a wave is in its cycle. A full cycle is 360°. Relative phase describes how far apart the two waves are in their cycles at the same position and time.

Keep the probe fixed and step the clock. The crest pattern travels to the right. The dot moves up and down at the same horizontal position. That sideways motion relative to the direction of travel is what makes the wave transverse.

What does the sum tell you?

Look at the two component plots at the same horizontal position. If both displacements point upward, they reinforce there. If one points upward and the other downward, their signed values subtract. Apply that same addition along the whole string to get the sum plot.

With equal waves half a cycle apart, every crest matches a trough of the same size. The sum is flat at every position and stays flat as time advances. With unequal waves half a cycle apart, the larger wave has some displacement left over after each subtraction.

Find a place where the sum crosses equilibrium while the sum wave still has crests. A zero at that single probe position is an instantaneous reading. Complete cancellation requires zero displacement throughout the wave.

Open the math and check the opening puzzle

Add the two signed string displacements at the same position and time. Then find the maximum distance of the resulting wave from equilibrium to get its amplitude.

y(x, t) = y1(x, t) + y2(x, t)

R = √(A12 + A22 + 2A1A2 cos(Δφ))

x, t
Position along the string in metres and elapsed time in seconds.
y1, y2, y
Signed instantaneous displacements of Wave 1, Wave 2, and their sum, in centimetres.
A1, A2, R
Nonnegative amplitudes of Wave 1, Wave 2, and their sum, in centimetres.
Δφ (delta phi)
Wave 2’s phase relative to Wave 1. The control uses degrees. The simulation converts the angle to radians for its trigonometry.

In this model both waves share the same moving phase pattern. Wave 2 adds a fixed angle to that pattern. Expanding its cosine separates the sum into a cosine part with coefficient A1 + A2 cos(Δφ) and a sine part with coefficient −A2 sin(Δφ). The amplitude is the square root of the sum of those coefficients squared, which gives the formula above.

For two waves with amplitude 1 cm, the squared amplitude is [2 + 2 cos(Δφ)] cm². Try the three equal-wave presets and compare them with these results.

From string displacement to probability

The numbers you added here were physical displacements in centimetres. Later we’ll add quantum probability amplitudes and use them to determine probabilities. Their phases can affect the result of that addition, but they have a different physical meaning from a string’s displacement.

Math and four missions

For source i, rᵢ is its distance to the probe and θᵢ = (2π/λ)rᵢ − 2πft + φᵢ, with f = 0.5 Hz. Each contribution is 0.50 cos θᵢ cm. With δ = (2π/λ)(r₂ − r₁) + φ₂ − φ₁, local amplitude is |cos(δ/2)| cm. Persistent cancellation requires matched arriving amplitudes and an odd multiple of π for δ.

Adjust geometry and screen probe

Math and four missions

The openings are at transverse coordinates −d/2 and +d/2. At screen coordinate y, r₁ and r₂ are their exact straight-line distances over the longitudinal distance D. Normalized time-averaged intensity is cos²[π(r₂ − r₁)/λ]. The estimate Δy ≈ λD/d applies near the axis at small angles. These classical intensities are a preview of interference ideas in quantum mechanics.
