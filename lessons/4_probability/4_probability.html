Lesson 04 · Coin lab

Probability

If heads has a 70% chance on every toss, what does that tell you about the next 10 tosses?

Predict what a ten-toss run can look like. Then compare fresh runs and ask which parts of your prediction apply to one run and which describe an average.

Define the experiment

Outcome
One result of one toss. This simulated coin gives H for heads or T for tails.
Sample space
The set of all possible outcomes we describe. For one toss, it is {H, T}.
Event
A set of outcomes we choose to count. For “heads on this toss,” the event is {H}.

The model probability of heads is p. The probability of tails is 1 − p. Tosses are independent: with the bias fixed, earlier results do not change the odds of the next toss.

    Worked example: observed frequency and expected count

    Imagine one run with 6 heads out of 10 and another with 8 out of 10. Their observed heads frequencies are 60% and 80%. These are example counts, separate from your simulated results.

    At p = 0.70, the expected count in ten tosses is 10 × 0.70 = 7 heads. Imagine writing 1 each time heads occurs and 0 each time tails occurs. The expected value of each entry is 0.70, and the ten expected values add to 7.

    Seven describes the average head count across repeated ten-toss runs. Every whole count from 0 through 10 is possible at 70%, and those counts do not all have the same probability. At 0% or 100% heads, the model gives only one possible head count.

    Step 1 · Find the complementary event

    The sample space for two ordered tosses is {HH, HT, TH, TT}. HT means heads first, tails second. “At least one H” is {HH, HT, TH}. Its complement is the event in which it fails: {TT}.

    Step 2 · Find the probability of two tails

    Each tail has probability 1 − p. Independence means the chance of tails on the second toss remains 1 − p after a tail on the first. Multiply the two probabilities to get P(TT) = (1 − p)².

    Listing four outcomes alone gives no reason to weight them equally. They each have probability ¼ here only when the coin is fair and the tosses are independent.

    Step 3 · Subtract from one and check at 70%

    The event and its complement together account for every pair. Their probabilities add to one.

    P(at least one H) = 1 − (1 − p)²

    At p = 0.70, two tails have probability 0.30 × 0.30 = 0.09. Subtract from one to get 0.91, or 91%. Sample pairs at 70% to see how a finite tally compares with that prediction.

    What happens after a streak?

    At the original 70% setting, suppose the coin produces four tails in a row. What chance does this model assign to heads next?

    Check your reasoning

    The chance is still 70%. The bias stays fixed, and the tosses are independent. The streak changes the observed tally. The model assigns the same probability to the next toss.

    Quantum theory also assigns probabilities to specified measurement outcomes. When we repeat the same preparation and measurement, we can compare those predictions with observed frequencies, as we did in the coin lab. Later lessons will explain where the quantum probabilities come from.
